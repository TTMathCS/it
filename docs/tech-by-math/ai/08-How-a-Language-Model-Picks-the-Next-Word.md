# 8. How a language model picks the next word

*A language model reads "The rover landed on" and has to say what comes next. Under all the talk of intelligence, each step is arithmetic you can do on paper: scores turned into probabilities by softmax, a loss that is minus the logarithm of one probability, attention that is a weighted average, learning that moves each score by "probability minus 1 or 0", and a size you can count from the shapes of its tables. This tutorial does each by hand and checks it with a program, using the published code of GPT-2 as the reference.*

!!! abstract "In this tutorial"
    **You will learn:**

    - softmax: how scores (logits) become probabilities, what temperature does, and why programs subtract the largest score first
    - cross-entropy loss and perplexity, and what "a loss of 10.8" means
    - scaled dot-product attention worked on three tokens, with the causal mask, and why the scores are divided by `√d`
    - why the gradient of softmax plus cross-entropy is simply `p − y`, one learning step by hand, and a tiny next-word model trained until it matches the counts
    - how to count a transformer's parameters, checked against all four GPT-2 sizes

    **You need:** `e^x` and `ln x`, sums, averages, and multiplying small tables of numbers. The Math site and the CS site's machine-learning books go deeper; this tutorial is a worked tour.

    **How to learn from it:** read 8.1 to 8.3, close the page, redo the examples of 8.3 on paper, then try the exercises in 8.7 before opening any answer. Plan about two and a half hours.

**About the examples.** No large model was run. Everything is computed with **numpy** (2.5) on small made-up numbers. The formulas are those of OpenAI's released GPT-2 code (`src/model.py` in `openai/gpt-2`): its `softmax`, its attention (`multihead_attn`) and the shapes of its weights; the parameter counts are checked against the sizes in GPT-2's model card. The gradient formula is checked against finite differences, a way of measuring slopes numerically. ## 8.1 The question

1. A model gives the scores 2, 1 and 0 to the words "Mars", "Earth" and "cheese". What are the probabilities, and what does turning the temperature down or up do to them?
2. The right word was "Earth". How wrong was the model, as a number?
3. Each word of a sentence looks back at the words before it. How is "looking back" computed?
4. How does the model change its scores after a mistake, and how many numbers does it have to change? GPT-2 small is said to have 124 million.

## 8.2 The idea: scores, probabilities, and small corrections

A language model splits text into **tokens** (words or pieces of words; GPT-2 has 50,257 of them). For the next position it computes one **score**, or **logit**, per token, and turns the scores into probabilities with **softmax**. Text is generated by picking a token from those probabilities, adding it, and repeating.

To compute the scores, each token is a list of `d` numbers (a **vector**; `d = 768` in GPT-2 small). Layers of **attention** let each position mix in information from earlier positions, and other layers transform each position on its own.

Training shows the model real text. At each position the **loss** measures how little probability it gave to the token that really came next, and every weight is moved a small step in the direction that lowers the loss. Each of these steps is short arithmetic.

## 8.3 The math by hand

### Step 1. Softmax and temperature

Softmax turns scores `z₁, …, zₙ` into probabilities:

```text
p_i = e^(z_i) / (e^(z_1) + … + e^(z_n))
```

Every `p_i` is positive and they add up to 1. For the scores 2, 1, 0:

```text
e^2 = 7.389,  e^1 = 2.718,  e^0 = 1,  sum 11.107
p = 7.389 / 11.107, 2.718 / 11.107, 1 / 11.107 = 0.665, 0.245, 0.090
```

A difference of 1 in score is a factor `e ≈ 2.72` in probability: "Mars" is 2.72 times as likely as "Earth".

**Temperature** `T` divides the scores before softmax. With `T = 0.5` the scores become 4, 2, 0: `e^4 = 54.60`, `e^2 = 7.389`, 1, sum 62.99, so `p = 0.867, 0.117, 0.016`. Low temperature sharpens the choice towards the top word; high temperature flattens it towards equal chances (Figure 8.1).

Adding the same number to every score changes nothing: the factor `e^c` appears above and below the line and cancels. Programs use this: GPT-2's `softmax` first subtracts the largest score. Without that, scores of 1000, 999 and 998 give `e^1000`, too big for a computer's floating-point numbers, and the result is "not a number"; after subtracting 1000 they become 0, −1, −2 and give 0.665, 0.245, 0.090 again (`ex01`, part 4).

### Step 2. Loss and perplexity

If the right word gets probability `p`, the **cross-entropy loss** is

```text
loss = −ln p
```

The right word was "Earth", with `p = 0.245`: loss `−ln 0.245 = 1.41`. A sure, right answer (`p = 1`) costs 0; `p = 0.5` costs 0.69; `p = 0.01` costs 4.61. The loss of a text is the average over its positions.

**Perplexity** is `e^(average loss)`. For a four-word sentence where the model gave the right words probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.8 and 0.1:

```text
losses 0.693, 1.386, 0.223, 2.303;  average 1.151;  perplexity e^1.151 = 3.16
```

Perplexity is `1 / (geometric mean of the probabilities)`: here `(0.5 × 0.25 × 0.8 × 0.1)^(1/4) = 0.01^(1/4) = 0.316`, and `1 / 0.316 = 3.16`. It reads as "as unsure as choosing among 3.16 equally likely words".

A model that guesses uniformly among GPT-2's 50,257 tokens has loss `ln 50,257 = 10.82` and perplexity 50,257. That is where an untrained model starts, roughly; training pushes the loss down from there.

### Step 3. Attention

Each position has three vectors made from its token: a **query** `q` (what it is looking for), a **key** `k` (what it offers) and a **value** `v` (what it passes on). Position `i` gives each earlier position `j` the score `q_i · k_j / √d` (a dot product, divided by the square root of the vector length), turns the scores into weights with softmax, and outputs the weighted average of the values. A **causal mask** hides later positions, so a token never sees the future.

Three tokens with `d = 2`:

```text
q1 = (1, 0)   k1 = (1, 0)   v1 = (10, 0)
q2 = (0, 1)   k2 = (0, 2)   v2 = (0, 10)
q3 = (1, 1)   k3 = (1, 1)   v3 = (5, 5)
```

Token 2 may look at tokens 1 and 2. Scores: `q2 · k1 = 0`, `q2 · k2 = 2`, divided by `√2`: 0 and 1.414. Softmax: `e^0 = 1`, `e^1.414 = 4.113`, sum 5.113, weights 0.196 and 0.804. Output: `0.196 × (10, 0) + 0.804 × (0, 10) = (1.96, 8.04)`. Token 2 mostly copies the value of token 2, whose key matches its query best.

Token 3 sees all three: scores `1, 2, 2` divided by `√2` are 0.707, 1.414, 1.414; weights 0.198, 0.401, 0.401; output `(3.98, 6.02)`. Token 1 sees only itself, so its output is `v1 = (10, 0)`. `ex03` gives the same three rows.

**Why divide by `√d`.** If the numbers in `q` and `k` are random with mean 0 and variance 1, the dot product is a sum of `d` such products, so its variance is `d`. For `d = 64`, scores would typically be about ±8, and softmax of such scores puts nearly all weight on one position; the model would struggle to learn. Dividing by `√d` brings the variance back to 1. `ex03` measures the variance at 2.00, 15.99, 64.07 and 255.50 for `d` = 2, 16, 64 and 256, and about 1 after scaling. With 16 keys and `d = 256`, the largest weight averages 0.93 without scaling and 0.25 with it.

### Step 4. Learning: p − y

Training needs, for each score `z_i`, how the loss changes when `z_i` changes: the **gradient**. For softmax followed by cross-entropy it is strikingly simple:

```text
∂ loss / ∂ z_i = p_i − y_i        (y_i = 1 for the right word, 0 for the others)
```

For our example (right word "Earth"): `p − y = (0.665, 0.245 − 1, 0.090) = (0.665, −0.755, 0.090)`. Raise the right word's score, lower the others, each in proportion to how wrong it was. `ex04` checks this against finite differences (nudging each score by a millionth and measuring the change in the loss); they agree to `10^−10`.

One step of **gradient descent** with learning rate 0.5 subtracts half the gradient:

```text
z = (2 − 0.333,  1 + 0.378,  0 − 0.045) = (1.667, 1.378, −0.045)
```

Now "Earth" has probability 0.388 instead of 0.245, and the loss falls from 1.41 to 0.95.

A real model has millions of weights between the input and the scores. The chain rule of calculus (**backpropagation**) carries `p − y` back through every layer to give each weight its own gradient; the step is the same.

`ex04` trains the smallest possible next-word model on one tiny text ("the cat sat on the mat . the cat ate the rat . the dog sat on the cat ."), one score for every pair of words. The loss falls from `ln 9 = 2.197` (nine different words, all equally likely) to 0.5665, next to the lowest possible 0.5658. After "the", the model learns the probabilities 0.50 for "cat" and 0.17 each for "mat", "rat" and "dog": exactly the fractions in the text (3 of 6 and 1 of 6). Minimising the cross-entropy loss makes the model's probabilities match how often things happen in its training text.

### Step 5. Counting parameters

The size of a model is the number of numbers in its tables. GPT-2's code gives every shape. One block with width `d`:

| Part | Shape | Count for `d = 768` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| layer norm 1 | `2d` | 1,536 |
| attention: q, k, v together | `d × 3d + 3d` | 1,771,776 |
| attention: output | `d × d + d` | 590,592 |
| layer norm 2 | `2d` | 1,536 |
| feed-forward in | `d × 4d + 4d` | 2,362,368 |
| feed-forward out | `4d × d + d` | 2,360,064 |
| **block** | `12d² + 13d` | **7,087,872** |

Add the token table (`50,257 × 768 = 38,597,376`), the position table (`1,024 × 768 = 786,432`), 12 blocks (`85,054,464`) and a final layer norm (1,536): **124,439,808**, the "124 million" of GPT-2's model card. The scores are computed by reusing the token table, so the output layer adds nothing. The same count for the larger sizes gives 354,823,168, 774,030,080 and 1,557,611,200: the model card's 355 million, 774 million and 1.5 billion. (The GPT-2 paper itself printed 117M and 345M for the two smaller sizes; the counts from the code are the ones above.)

For large models `12 L d²` is most of it: in GPT-2 XL the blocks are 95% of the parameters. A common rule of thumb (Kaplan and others, 2020) puts training compute at about `6 × parameters × tokens` floating-point operations: for 124 million parameters and 10 billion tokens, about `7.5 × 10^18`.

## 8.4 Why it is true

**Softmax gives probabilities.** Each `e^z` is positive, and dividing by their sum makes them add to 1. Shifting all scores by `c` multiplies every `e^z` by `e^c`, which cancels in the division.

**Temperature.** The ratio of two probabilities is `e^((z_i − z_j) / T)`. Small `T` makes every ratio huge, so the top word takes almost all; large `T` makes every ratio close to 1.

**Perplexity is a geometric mean.** `e^(−(ln p₁ + … + ln pₙ)/n) = (p₁ × … × pₙ)^(−1/n)`, because the exponential turns the sum of logarithms back into a product.

**The variance of a dot product.** For independent `q_i`, `k_i` with mean 0 and variance 1, each product has mean 0 and variance `1 × 1 = 1`, and variances of independent terms add, so the sum of `d` of them has variance `d`. Dividing by `√d` divides the variance by `d`.

**The gradient `p − y`.** The loss is `−z_t + ln(e^(z_1) + … + e^(z_n))` for the right word `t`. The derivative of the second part with respect to `z_i` is `e^(z_i) / sum = p_i`; the first part contributes −1 only for `i = t`. Together, `p_i − y_i`.

**Training matches the counts.** For one previous word, the loss summed over the text is `−Σ_b count(b) × ln p(b)`. Among all probability lists, this is smallest when `p(b) = count(b) / total` (Gibbs' inequality), so gradient descent heads there, as `ex04` shows.

**The parameter count** is a sum of products of shapes; `12d²` comes from `3d² + d² + 4d² + 4d²`.

## 8.6 Where it breaks

- **Probabilities are not truth.** A model's probabilities match patterns in its training text (step 4), not facts. A fluent wrong answer can have high probability.
- **Temperature is not the only knob.** Real systems also cut off unlikely tokens (top-k, top-p) and may add other rules; the examples here sample from the plain softmax.
- **Real models are not GPT-2.** Newer models change many details (other position schemes, other normalisations, mixtures of experts, larger vocabularies). The steps of this tutorial, softmax, cross-entropy, scaled attention and `p − y`, are common to them; their exact designs and sizes, when not published, are unknown here.
- **Many heads.** GPT-2 splits each attention layer into 12 heads of 64 numbers each, so `√d` there is `√64 = 8`, the head size, not `√768`. The parameter count is the same either way.
- **Training is more than one formula.** Real training uses optimisers such as Adam, learning-rate schedules and huge batches; the rule `6 × parameters × tokens` is an estimate, not a measurement.
- **Rounding.** Probabilities like `10^−30` underflow to 0 in floating point; programs work with logarithms (log-softmax) to avoid `ln 0`.

## 8.7 Exercises

Do 1 to 6 with a pencil. 7 needs a little care. 8 is code.

**E1. Scores 3, 3, 0 for three words. Give the probabilities.**

??? success "Answer"
    `e^3 = 20.09` twice and `e^0 = 1`: sum 41.17. Probabilities `0.488, 0.488, 0.024`. Equal scores, equal probabilities.

**E2. Scores 1 and 0 for two words. Find the probabilities at `T = 1` and at `T = 0.1`.**

??? success "Answer"
    `T = 1`: `e / (e + 1) = 2.718 / 3.718 = 0.731` and 0.269. `T = 0.1`: scores 10 and 0, `e^10 / (e^10 + 1) = 22,026 / 22,027 = 0.99995`, and 0.00005.

**E3. A model gives the right words of a three-word text the probabilities 0.5, 0.5 and 0.125. Find the average loss and the perplexity.**

??? success "Answer"
    Losses `0.693, 0.693, 2.079`; average `3.466 / 3 = 1.155`. Perplexity `e^1.155 = 3.17`; or `1 / (0.5 × 0.5 × 0.125)^(1/3) = 1 / (1/32)^(1/3) = 32^(1/3) = 3.17`.

**E4. With the keys and values of step 3, a fourth token has query `q4 = (0, 0)`. What is its output?**

??? success "Answer"
    All four scores are 0 (including its own key, whatever it is), so each weight is 1/4, and the output is the plain average of the four values. With `v4 = (0, 0)`: `((10 + 0 + 5 + 0) / 4, (0 + 10 + 5 + 0) / 4) = (3.75, 3.75)`. A query of zeros looks at everything equally.

**E5. Scores 0, 0, 0 and the right word is the first. Give the gradient, and the scores after one step with learning rate 3.**

??? success "Answer"
    `p = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)`, `p − y = (−2/3, 1/3, 1/3)`. New scores `(0 + 2, 0 − 1, 0 − 1) = (2, −1, −1)`. Probabilities: `e^2 = 7.389`, `e^−1 = 0.368` twice, sum 8.125: `(0.909, 0.045, 0.045)`.

**E6. Count the parameters of one block with `d = 1024`. Check against `12d² + 13d`.**

??? success "Answer"
    `12 × 1,048,576 + 13 × 1,024 = 12,582,912 + 13,312 = 12,596,224`. GPT-2 medium has 24 of them: 302,309,376 in its blocks.

**E7. Why is the vector of gradients `p − y` always made of numbers that add up to 0? What does that mean for the scores?**

??? success "Answer"
    The `p_i` add to 1 and the `y_i` add to 1, so `p − y` adds to 0. A step therefore never shifts all the scores up or down together; and a shift of all scores would not change the probabilities anyway (step 1). Only the differences between scores matter, and only they are changed.

**E8. (Code.) Train the tiny model of step 4 on another text and check that, after training, the probabilities after one word match the counts.**

??? success "Answer"
    Change the `text = ...` line in [ex04_learning.py](src/08/ex04_learning.py) and run it again. For the given text, after "the": learned 0.4999, 0.1665, 0.1665, 0.1665 against counted 0.5000, 0.1667, 0.1667, 0.1667 ([output](src/08/ex04.out.txt)). With more steps the gap shrinks further; it never becomes exactly 0, because a probability of exactly 0 for unseen pairs would need a score of minus infinity.

## Glossary

- **Token**: a word or piece of a word; the unit a language model reads and writes.
- **Logit (score)**: the number a model gives each possible next token before softmax.
- **Softmax**: `e^(z_i)` divided by the sum of all `e^z`; turns scores into probabilities.
- **Temperature**: a divisor of the scores; low sharpens, high flattens.
- **Cross-entropy loss**: `−ln` of the probability given to the right token.
- **Perplexity**: `e^(average loss)`; the effective number of equally likely choices.
- **Attention**: each position's output as a weighted average of earlier positions' values, weighted by softmax of query-key dot products.
- **Causal mask**: hiding later positions, so the model cannot see the future.
- **Gradient**: how much the loss changes per unit change of each number; here `p − y` for the scores.
- **Gradient descent**: moving every number a small step against its gradient.
- **Parameter**: one learned number in a model's tables.
